# Iraq’s Prime Minister Vows to Disarm and Disband Iran-Backed Militias in 2027

Ali al-Zaidi is a political neophyte hoping to succeed where previous leaders failed. In an interview, he laid out an ambitious timeline that could prove hard to meet.

![Image 1: A bearded person in a blue suit and bright blue necktie is seated in a cream chair with an Iraqi flag pin in his lapel. ](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/21/multimedia/21int-iraq-pm-1-vmqz/21int-iraq-pm-1-vmqz-articleLarge.jpg?quality=75&auto=webp&disable=upscale)

Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi of Iraq during a meeting with President Trump in the Oval Office in July.Credit...Doug Mills/The New York Times

By [Erika Solomon](https://www.nytimes.com/by/erika-solomon) and Falih Hassan

Reporting from Baghdad

Sept. 21, 2026

[See more of our coverage in your search results.Encuentra más de nuestra cobertura en los resultados de búsqueda.Add The New York Times on Google Agrega The New York Times en Google](https://www.google.com/preferences/source?cs=0&hl=en&q=nytimes.com)

Iraq’s prime minister, Ali al-Zaidi, pledged to disarm his country’s Iran-backed militias by June 2027, laying out an exceptionally ambitious timeline for bringing the country’s unwieldy array of militants under state control.

The phased disarmament plan, which Mr. al-Zaidi laid out for the first time in an interview with The New York Times on Saturday in Baghdad, would be central to how Iraq manages the delicate balancing act between its two most important allies, the United States and Iran, at a time when those countries are at war.

Iraq is under mounting pressure from the Trump administration, its most critical security and economic partner, to defang the Iran-aligned forces, which have accrued not only military might, but also political power. At the same time, Iran, Iraq’s neighbor, is seeking to deepen its influence in the country and expand the use of those militias in the war with Washington.

Disarming the militias has bedeviled previous Iraqi leaders, including those with far more experience than Mr. al-Zaidi in the country’s fractious political scene. Iran-backed factions have become increasingly entrenched over recent years, [forming parties that are enmeshed in Iraq’s political and economic system](http://nytimes.com/2025/11/11/world/middleeast/iraq-parliamentary-election-vote.html) and making their containment a daunting task.

“This is not something that is optional. It is a necessity,” Mr. al-Zaidi said of his disarmament plan, in his first interview with foreign news media at his official residence. “Others who spoke of this then backtracked,” he said. “They yielded to pressure or to other concerns. For me, this issue, along with that of corruption, is a matter of honor.”

The difficulties have not deterred Mr. al-Zaidi, who was a political novice before becoming prime minister this year. Before his nomination, the wealthy businessman was [largely unknown to most Iraqis](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/28/world/middleeast/iraq-ali-al-zaidi.html).

This week, as Mr. al-Zaidi will attend his first United Nations General Assembly, he is confronting not only a security challenge, but also a daunting economic crisis.

Iraq, the world’s third-largest supplier of oil, has lost 60 percent of its monthly export revenue because of the war between the United States and Iran, which has choked off shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, Mr. al-Zaidi said. Since taking office, he has been pitching proposals to build pipelines that would bypass the Persian Gulf, including one that he said could reach Europe.

“We wish to build bridges and economic ties between Iraq and the countries of the world,” he said. “Under this government, Iraq will be a meeting point, not a point of hostility.”

Realizing these aspirations, however, will depend on Mr. al-Zaidi’s meeting the Trump administration’s demand that he rein in the militias.

“Arms have to be confined first so you can build a solid economy,” he said.

Image

![Image 2: Soldiers in camouflage uniforms wearing helmets and carrying rifles walk down the middle of a road. Two lines of dark security vehicles drive on either side.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/09/21/multimedia/21int-iraq-pm-3-wlbm/21int-iraq-pm-3-wlbm-articleLarge.jpg?quality=75&auto=webp&disable=upscale)

Members from Iraq’s Popular Mobilization Forces attended the funeral of fighters from Kataib Hezbollah who were killed in a U.S. airstrike in Babil Province in March.Credit...Hadi Mizban/Associated Press

Mr. al-Zaidi initially announced in the summer that the militias would be disarmed by the time the last remaining U.S. forces in Iraq withdraw on Sept. 30. Iraqi militias immediately rejected that timeline. After that, officials tried to correct what was seen in Iraq as a political blunder, saying instead that disarmament could begin Sept. 30.

Mr. al-Zaidi’s government has since formed a committee to negotiate with the militias over disarmament. He said the terms of the agreement on the timeline would be announced on Sept. 30.

According to Mr. al-Zaidi, there would first be a 90-day period during which the militias would not launch any attacks and be assured that they would not be attacked by U.S. forces. After that, the militias would begin handing over their weapons, with disarmament ending by June 30, 2027, he said, though the militias are still pushing to have until the end of 2027 to disarm.

[SKIP ADVERTISEMENT](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/21/world/middleeast/iraq-prime-minister-interview-disarm-militias.html?unlocked_article_code=1&smid=nytcore-ios-share#after-story-ad-3)

Regional diplomats and some Iraqi security officials warn the timeline is extremely precarious. Most described it as nearly impossible to meet during a time of war, when Iran’s militia allies have proved valuable partners in sowing regional chaos and helping impose drastic costs on the global economy.

The militias have struck repeatedly at neighboring Kuwait and Jordan, as well as energy sites in Iraq and the U.S. Embassy in Baghdad.

This month, Saudi Arabia accused Iraq of being the source of a drone attack that knocked out the operation of the kingdom’s east-west pipeline, which provides 4 percent of global oil supplies. Iraqi officials believe splinter cells of the Iraqi militias helped operatives facilitate, though not orchestrate, that attack.

“The Iraqi government has stated that they’re not part of this war,” said Victoria Taylor, director of the Iraq Initiative at the Washington-based Atlantic Council. “But they don’t really have control over that.”

Iran would not greenlight the disarming of its Iraqi allies, she said, at a time when they have proved themselves to be “an extremely potent tool.”

A senior Iraqi security official, who spoke on the condition of anonymity to discuss sensitive negotiations with militias, warned that although some U.S. officials seemed receptive to Mr. al-Zaidi’s timeline, the plan would fail without a peace deal between the United States and Iran. That is still nowhere in sight.

Beyond the geopolitics, disarming Iraq’s militias could prove difficult because they have become so numerous and diffuse.

After U.S. forces invaded Iraq in 2003 and toppled its dictator, Saddam Hussein, several militant groups from Iran’s Shiite Muslim majority emerged to fight the U.S. occupation, often with training and arms from Iran, the region’s Shiite power.

Then, in 2014, the Sunni jihadist group the Islamic State launched an offensive that at its height seized more than a third of Iraqi territory. While awaiting help from a U.S.-led military coalition, Iraq announced a mobilization of forces. Countless new armed groups were formed, many under Iranian direction.

Iraq’s government has tried since then to rein in these militias — collectively called the Popular Mobilization Forces, or P.M.F. — and place them under state authority. Even those who have come under the state, however, maintain deep ties with Tehran and other Iran-backed militias outside state control.

Image

![Image 3: A coffin wrapped in the green, white and red colors of the Iranian flag is held overhead by a crowd. Some people hold up yellow flags.](https://static01.nyt.com/images/2026/07/31/multimedia/21int-iraq-pm4/31IRAN-IRAQ-PRINT-mbvf-articleLarge.jpg?quality=75&auto=webp&disable=upscale)

A funeral for those killed in airstrikes on bases belonging to the Popular Mobilization Forces in Mosul in July.Credit...Hadi Mizban/Associated Press

The government’s current efforts are focused on four powerful militias that have resisted calls to disarm or come under the state.

Mr. al-Zaidi said his goal is for these militias to cease to exist as independent entities. “They will join the Popular Mobilization Forces as individuals and integrate into them,” he said. After the deadline, he said, any groups operating under militia names “will be deemed outlaws.”

These main groups insist they have the right to bear arms as long as “occupying forces” pose a threat, and point to the war the United States and Israel launched on Iran in February. The groups argue that U.S. forces and Israel, [which built covert bases in the Iraqi desert](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/17/world/europe/israel-iraq-iran-bases.html), are using Iraq’s territory.

Ramzy Mardini, the founder of Geopol Labs, a Middle East-based geopolitical risk advisory firm, said a successful disarmament timeline required not only an easing of the regional conflict but a government that could enforce its demands and offer incentives or assurances to armed factions that comply.

[SKIP ADVERTISEMENT](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/21/world/middleeast/iraq-prime-minister-interview-disarm-militias.html?unlocked_article_code=1&smid=nytcore-ios-share#after-story-ad-6)

“Until there are real changes in the political and security context, a deadline is merely an aspiration,” he said.

As the final U.S. troop withdrawal has neared, militias have raised additional conditions for their disarmament. The groups, for example, have pointed to the continued presence of Turkish troops in northern Iraq. Turkey has said it must maintain forces in Iraq to fight Turkish Kurdish separatist militants on its borders. In response, Mr. al-Zaidi said he has sought a deal with Turkey for its withdrawal.

In the past, Turkey has said any withdrawal is conditional on Kurdish militants’ camps and weapons being cleared from northern Iraq. Mr. al-Zaidi said his government was also working on a timeline to absorb local Kurdish forces into Iraq’s P.M.F. to satisfy that demand.

In pursuit of a deal with Turkey, Mr. al-Zaidi said he had leveraged Iraq’s efforts at boosting economic ties between the countries. This year, Iraq agreed to a one-year deal to increase oil exports to Turkey via an existing Iraqi pipeline, and Iraq now aims to increase the pipeline’s capacity, he said. Last week, during visits to France and Germany, he proposed plans to build pipelines via Turkey that would bring Iraqi oil directly to Europe.

During the interview, Mr. al-Zaidi also detailed some of the economic challenges his country now faced. Iraq has lost approximately $60 billion in revenues since the war began, he said.

Although Iraq considers itself a regional partner with Iran, he said, Iran has not allowed Iraqi tankers to pass through the Strait of Hormuz.

“We spoke at length. The decision emerging from Iran is firm,” he added. “Their goal is to drive up global oil prices. This is not about being close to them or not. Regardless, they want to create pressure.”
